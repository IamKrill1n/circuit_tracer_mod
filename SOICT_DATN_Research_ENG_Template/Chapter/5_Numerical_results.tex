\section{Overview}
The previous chapters defined the summarization method and analyzed its properties on
paper. This chapter tests the central claim empirically: that the summary graph is
\emph{faithful}, meaning its supernode edges reflect genuine causal dependencies in the
replacement model rather than artifacts of clustering. It first describes the datasets
(Section~\ref{sec:datasets}) and experimental setup (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), then defines
the evaluation methodology (Section~\ref{sec:eval-method}) and baselines
(Section~\ref{sec:baselines}). The main result is a steering-intervention study that grades
the clustering and edge-construction stages together (Section~\ref{sec:intervention-results});
a second, in-progress evaluation using an LLM to score supernode relevance is reported in
draft (Section~\ref{sec:llm-results}).

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}
\subsection{Synthetic analogies from BATS}
The Bigger Analogy Test Set (BATS) is a benchmark for probing the relational and semantic
knowledge of word embeddings and language models. We extract the entities in its
lexicography category to build a synthetic dataset of ten prompts per subcategory. Each
prompt has the form ``The saying goes: $A$ is to $B$ as $C$ is to $D$'', where $A$, $B$,
$C$, $D$ are entities of one subcategory, and the task is to predict $D$ from $A$, $B$,
and $C$.

\subsection{Multi-hop reasoning}
We follow the multi-hop queries of Ameisen et al.~\cite{ameisen2025circuit}. Each query
requires reasoning across several steps to reach the answer and so tests the model's
ability to chain basic inferences. In both datasets we keep only prompts the model answers
correctly with confidence above $20\%$, so every attribution graph explains a prediction
the model actually makes.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\subsection{Environment}
Experiments run on a single workstation with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU (24\,GB
VRAM) and 64\,GB of RAM, under Ubuntu 22.04 LTS. All code uses Python 3.10.

\subsection{Tools and libraries}
We generate the attribution graphs with the circuit-tracing library of Ameisen et
al.~\cite{ameisen2025circuit}, reuse its feature metadata, and adapt its viewer to
visualize the summary graphs.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:eval-method}
\subsection{Steering-intervention faithfulness}
A summary graph is faithful only if its supernode edges reflect genuine causal
dependencies. We test this directly with a steering intervention: we perturb one
supernode at its source and check whether every other supernode moves in the direction
its incoming edge predicts. The test grades the clustering partition together with the
edges $W_H$ at once, so it scores the summary graph as a whole rather than any single
stage.

\paragraph{Intervention protocol.}
For each non-fixed supernode $C$ we negate every member feature, setting its activation
to $M \cdot a$ with steer factor $M = -1$ and $a$ the original activation, then run one
forward pass with all members of $C$ steered together. The perturbation is applied as
\emph{constrained} (direct-effect) patching, following Ameisen et
al.~\cite{ameisen2025circuit}: each steered feature writes its decoder vector only over
the layer window $[l, l+1]$ around its own layer $l$ --- its own-layer reconstruction
plus the first cross-layer write --- while the attention patterns and LayerNorm scales
are frozen at their clean values. Freezing attention and normalization isolates the
\emph{direct} effect that the summary edges model and suppresses the indirect paths the
attribution graph does not represent.

\paragraph{Measured response.}
After the intervention we read the activation change $\Delta a_{C'}$ of every other
feature supernode $C'$ and the probability change $\Delta p_{C'}$ of every logit
supernode and of the target token. For a steered source $C$ and a downstream supernode
$C'$ joined by the summary edge $W_{H,\,C \to C'}$, the edge \emph{predicts} that the
downstream response carries the sign
$\operatorname{sgn}(W_{H,\,C \to C'}) \cdot \operatorname{sgn}(\Delta a_C)$, where
$\Delta a_C$ is the activation change of the steered supernode itself (negative under
negation).

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We summarize each graph with three quantities, all oriented so that higher is better.
\emph{Edge sign-consistency} is the fraction of summary edges whose measured downstream
sign matches the predicted one, weighted by edge magnitude:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SC} \;=\;
\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{(C, C') \in E_H} |W_{H,\,C \to C'}| \cdot
      \mathbf{1}\!\bigl[\operatorname{sgn}(\Delta m_{C'}) =
      \operatorname{sgn}(W_{H,\,C \to C'})\,\operatorname{sgn}(\Delta a_C)\bigr]}
     {\displaystyle\sum_{(C, C') \in E_H} |W_{H,\,C \to C'}|}
\;\in\; [0, 1],
\label{eq:sign-consistency}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta m_{C'}$ is $\Delta a_{C'}$ for a feature target and $\Delta p_{C'}$ for a
logit target; $\mathrm{SC} = 1$ means every edge fires in the predicted direction.
\emph{Monotonicity} is the Spearman correlation $\rho$ between the edge magnitude
$|W_{H,\,C \to C'}|$ and the measured effect magnitude over the feature-to-feature edges,
testing whether heavier edges produce larger downstream changes. \emph{Output effect} is
the mean absolute target-token probability shift $|\Delta p|$ across all single-supernode
interventions, measuring how much causal weight a supernode carries toward the model's
answer.

\subsection{LLM scoring}
The second evaluation uses a large language model to label each supernode and then score
its relevance to the prediction on a $[0, 1]$ scale, with $0$ meaning irrelevant and $1$
highly relevant to the final answer. The labeling and scoring prompts are kept separate so
that the score does not simply restate the label. This evaluation is still in progress; its
draft results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:llm-results}.

\section{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
We compare the exact ILP clusterer (Section~\ref{sec:objective}) against a $K$-means
baseline that partitions the same role vectors $r(u)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:role} with
Euclidean $K$-means. The ILP chooses its supernode count $K$ endogenously, whereas
$K$-means takes $K$ as input; we therefore set the baseline's $K$ equal to the ILP's $K$
on each graph, so the two methods differ only in how they assign features and not in
granularity, making the comparison fair at a fixed budget. The study runs on the $100$
pruned attribution graphs from the analogies dataset (softmax token weighting, pruning
$\alpha = 0.5$, node threshold $\eta_V = 0.02$). Each graph is processed once: the
intervention is deterministic given a partition, the ILP is solved to optimality
($\theta = 0$, $\lambda = 1$, layer span $\le 4$, $30$\,s time limit), and $K$-means uses
$20$ restarts under a fixed seed.

\section{Steering-intervention results}
\label{sec:intervention-results}
Both clusterers produce causally faithful summary graphs: negating a supernode moves its
downstream neighbours in the predicted direction for the large majority of edges, and the
effect grows with edge weight. Table~\ref{tab:intervention} reports the two methods at
equal granularity.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\caption{Steering-intervention faithfulness on the $100$ analogy graphs, ILP versus a
$K$-matched $K$-means baseline. Values are means over the $100$ graphs; arrows mark the
direction of improvement and bold the better method per column. The three causal columns
--- sign-consistency $\mathrm{SC}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:sign-consistency}), monotonicity $\rho$,
and output effect $|\Delta p|$ --- all favour $K$-means, and every per-graph difference is
significant (paired Wilcoxon $p < 0.01$). The right-most column is diagnostic: the mean
number of supernode edges left visible in $H$, a measure of exposed structure rather than
of quality. $K$ is identical for the two methods by construction.}
\label{tab:intervention}
\begin{tabular}{l c c c c c}
\toprule
Clusterer & $K$ & $\mathrm{SC}\uparrow$ & $\rho\uparrow$ & $|\Delta p|\uparrow$ & edges/graph \\
\midrule
ILP ($\lambda = 1$)            & 15.3 & 0.878 & 0.442 & 0.175 & 14.6 \\
$K$-means (role vectors)       & 15.3 & \textbf{0.941} & \textbf{0.539} & \textbf{0.190} & 13.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

At equal $K$ the $K$-means baseline is the more faithful of the two on every causal
metric. Its edges are more sign-consistent ($0.941$ vs.\ $0.878$), more monotone
($\rho = 0.539$ vs.\ $0.442$), and its supernodes shift the target probability more
($0.190$ vs.\ $0.175$); all three gaps hold across the $100$ graphs at $p < 0.01$. The
pooled, edge-weighted sign-consistency over all $4{,}799$ tested supernode pairs tells the
same story ($0.927$ vs.\ $0.861$).

The one axis on which the ILP leads is the amount of causal structure it exposes: it
leaves $14.6$ supernode edges visible per graph against the baseline's $13.0$. This is the
intended effect of the causal-preservation loss $L_{\text{causal}}$ (D3), which penalizes
merging directly connected features and so keeps their edge in $H$ instead of absorbing it
into a supernode. The same mechanism explains the sign-consistency gap: the ILP surfaces
more --- and individually weaker --- edges and is then graded on all of them, whereas
$K$-means hides its weakest, most ambiguous edges inside clusters and is graded only on the
strong ones that remain. The comparison therefore measures per-edge faithfulness at fixed
$K$, not the overall quality of the abstraction, and it is partly biased toward the method
that exposes less structure.

Two caveats further temper the result. First, $K$-means optimizes role-vector cohesion
directly, which is close to what the steering metric rewards, so it is partly graded on its
own objective. Second, the comparison fixes the trade-off weight at $\lambda = 1$, and that
weight is precisely the knob moving the ILP along the structure-versus-cohesion axis; a
sweep over $\lambda$ is needed to locate the operating point at which the two effects
balance. We conclude that both abstractions are faithful and that, at this single operating
point, the simple baseline is at least as faithful per edge while the ILP retains more of
the circuit's causal wiring.

\section{LLM-score evaluation (draft)}
\label{sec:llm-results}
This evaluation is in progress and reported in draft. The intended comparison contrasts the
LLM relevance score of each supernode (Section~\ref{sec:eval-method}) with a human
relevance judgment on the same supernodes, so that agreement between the two would justify
using the LLM score as a scalable proxy for human-judged supernode quality. The protocol,
the prompt design, and the human-rating procedure are fixed; the rated graphs and the
agreement statistics are still being collected, so no quantitative claim is made here. We
report this evaluation in full in a later revision.

\section{Chapter Summary}
This chapter validated the summary graph empirically. The steering intervention confirmed
that both the exact ILP clusterer and the $K$-means baseline yield causally faithful
abstractions, with downstream responses matching the sign and magnitude of the summary
edges on the large majority of pairs across $100$ analogy graphs. The two methods trade
off along a single axis at the tested operating point: $K$-means is more faithful per edge,
while the ILP exposes more of the circuit's causal wiring through its causal-preservation
loss. The result also identified the next step --- a sweep over the trade-off weight
$\lambda$ --- and an evaluation still under way, the LLM-versus-human relevance comparison.
The final chapter discusses what these findings mean and where the method falls short.
