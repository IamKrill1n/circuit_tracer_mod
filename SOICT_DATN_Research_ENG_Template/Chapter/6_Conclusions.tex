\section{Summary}
This thesis addressed the challenge of reading an attribution graph for circuit tracing task. It formalized the problem of summarizing an attribution graph and
proposed an automated pipeline for producing compact, human-readable summary
graphs. 

The proposed method has three stages: prunning, clustering, and visualizing. The first stage, prunning, discards unimportant nodes and edges, leaving a smaller subgraph. The second stage, clustering, groups features with similar causal roles into supernodes. The third stage, visualizing, aggregates the clustered graph into a directed acyclic summary graph and assigns labels to its supernodes.
The output is a compact summary graph, enabling researchers to inspect the causal story of the model's computation at a glance.

Extensive experiments validated the faithfulness and interpretability of the
summary graphs. The intervention experiments show that the summaries produce
interpretable and steerable supernodes, and that the edges between them correctly
predict the direction and relative strength of measured intervention responses.
Additional evaluations of intermediate results show that the proposed pruning
method outperforms influence-only pruning along the faithfulness--interpretability
axis, and that the proposed clustering method outperforms classical clustering
baselines along the causal-information-preservation--atomicity axis.

However, this approach still has several limitations. The first is the use of
SHAP values as a proxy for human rationale at the input-token level. Normalizing
SHAP values helps mask uninformative tokens, but this thesis provides no
theoretical analysis of why SHAP should align better with human rationale than
influence. Second,
the experiments did not extend to longer prompts or a broader range of models
because of computational resource limitations. Third, this thesis did not
propose a method to evaluate the quality of the supernode labels, yet we found that even imperfect labels can help us understand the flow of information in the summary graph.
Finally, this thesis lacked a end to end baseline for summarization since there are no existing summarization methods for CLT attribution graphs.

Despite these limitations, the applications of this thesis work is limitless. First, it directly enables human researchers to scale the study the biology of LLMs. Second, it can be used to debug the model behavior, namely chain of thoughts faithfulness,
jailbreaks, halucinations, ... 
\section{Future Work}

Future work should strengthen both the method and its evaluation. A first
direction is to replace the current SHAP-based relevance proxy with a relevance
measure defined directly on the attribution graph, so that upstream and
downstream importance are expressed in the same linear framework. A second
direction is to evaluate the pipeline on more models, tasks, and prompt types,
especially cases where circuits involve longer reasoning chains or multiple
competing answers. A third direction is to improve label validation, either by
measuring whether supernode labels predict member-feature behavior or by using
intervention results to test whether a labeled supernode behaves as a single
mechanism. Finally, a future work is to compare the proposed method with other methods once they are published.
