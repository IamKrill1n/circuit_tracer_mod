\section{Summary}

This thesis addressed the problem of making attribution graphs from circuit tracing
more readable without discarding their causal content. Attribution graphs provide a
prompt-specific account of how a language model produces a prediction, but they
are often too large and dense for direct inspection. To address this limitation, the
thesis formalized the problem of attribution-graph summarization and proposed an
automated method for producing compact, human-readable summary graphs.

The proposed method consists of three stages: pruning, clustering, and interpreting.
The pruning stage removes nodes and edges that are unlikely to participate in the
selected input-to-output computation. Instead of using only downstream influence
on the target logit, it combines downstream influence with upstream relevance from
the selected input tokens, so that retained nodes must be both reached from the
prompt context and relevant to the prediction. The clustering stage groups retained
feature nodes into supernodes. It formulates clustering as an optimization problem
that rewards atomicity, so that features inside a supernode have similar signed
causal roles, while also preserving visible causal structure between supernodes. The
interpreting stage aggregates the clustered graph into a directed acyclic summary
graph and assigns labels and roles to the resulting supernodes. The final output is
therefore a smaller graph that presents the model's computation as a sequence of
interpretable causal steps.

The empirical results provide evidence that the summary graphs satisfy the main
desiderata proposed in this thesis. The pruning experiments show that adding
upstream relevance to the importance score preserves replacement score and graph
completeness at matched graph sizes, while filtering out features that are less
semantically connected to the selected prompt tokens. The clustering experiments
show that the proposed ILP clusterer gives stronger causal-structure preservation
than the tested clustering baselines, as measured by lower internalized edge mass
and lower DAG loss, while still maintaining coherent role-vector clusters. The
intervention experiments further validate the final summaries. Entity-swap steering
shows that Output supernodes often contain answer-specific information that can be
suppressed or transferred across prompts. Together, these results suggest that the proposed
pipeline compresses attribution graphs while preserving important parts of their
causal structure.

The approach nevertheless has several limitations. First, the relevance score uses
SHAP values as a proxy for input-token importance. Although this helps suppress
uninformative prompt tokens in practice, SHAP is a non-linear attribution method and it is not clear how it can transfer to the linear attribution graphs. Second, the experiments are
limited in scale. Therefore, the thesis only uses SHAP to approximate human rationales and not for evaluations. They focus mainly on analogy and multi-hop reasoning prompts
for one circuit-tracing backbone, and do not test longer prompts, larger models, or
a broader range of behaviors. Third, there is no
established end-to-end baseline for summarizing CLT attribution graphs. The thesis
therefore compares against stage-level baselines such as influence-only pruning and
classical clustering methods, but it cannot yet compare against a mature competing
summarization pipeline. Finally, the method inherits limitations from the underlying
replacement model: if important behavior is represented in reconstruction error,
attention dynamics, or features not captured well by the transcoder, the summary
graph may omit or distort part of the original model computation.

Despite these limitations, the thesis provides a concrete step toward scalable
mechanistic analysis of language models. It replaces manual pruning and manual
grouping with explicit objectives, produces summary graphs that can be inspected
directly, and validates those summaries using causal interventions. This makes
attribution-graph analysis more systematic and opens the possibility of applying
circuit-tracing tools to larger collections of prompts. In the long term, methods of
this kind could support the study of model internals, the diagnosis of unexpected
model behavior, and the investigation of failure modes such as hallucination,
jailbreak susceptibility, and unfaithful reasoning traces.

\section{Future Work}

Future work should strengthen both the summarization method and its evaluation.
A first direction is to replace the current SHAP-based relevance proxy with a
relevance measure that align better with the attribution graph. Such a measure would
express upstream and downstream importance within the same linear framework, while approximate human rationales closely.

A second direction is to evaluate the pipeline on a broader set of models, prompts,
and tasks. The present experiments focus on controlled analogy and multi-hop
reasoning settings. Future evaluations should include longer prompts, tasks with
multiple competing answers,  and
models with different sizes or replacement-model quality. This would clarify which
parts of the pipeline are robust and which depend on the structure of the current
experimental setting.

A third direction is to develop stronger end-to-end baselines. Since no standard
method currently exists for CLT attribution-graph summarization, future work
could compare against expert-written summaries, adapted neuron-circuit clustering
methods, ADAG-style attribution-profile clustering, or ablations of the proposed
pipeline. Such comparisons would make it easier to separate the contribution of
each stage from the contribution of the complete pipeline.

Finally, future work should use the proposed pipeline to study the biology of language models, investigate failure modes, and build more reliable evidence about model behavior.
