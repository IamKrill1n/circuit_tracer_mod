\section{Overview}
The previous chapter validated the summary graph empirically; this chapter steps back to
discuss what the findings mean, where the method falls short, and what they amount to as a
whole. It first weighs the results and the method's limitations, then summarizes the
thesis's contributions and outcomes and points to future work.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The empirical results show that attribution-graph summarization can reduce graph
size while preserving much of the computation needed for interpretation.
Influence--relevance pruning keeps graph-level faithfulness close to the
influence-only baseline while giving the method an explicit way to prefer nodes
connected to the input tokens. The ILP clusterer gives stronger downstream-effect
coherence and lower structural loss than the clustering baselines, which
suggests that the resulting supernodes preserve visible causal structure better.
The steering evaluation further supports this conclusion: the final summary
graphs have high sign consistency and positive edge-effect rank correlation, so
their signed edges often predict the direction and relative strength of measured
intervention responses.

The method still has several limitations. First, the relevance score is computed
using SHAP-style attribution, while the attribution graph itself represents
linear feature-to-feature effects inside the replacement model. This mismatch
means that relevance should be interpreted as a proxy for upstream input
dependence, not as the same quantity as graph flow. Second, the quantitative
evaluation focuses mainly on analogy prompts, with multi-hop reasoning used as a
qualitative stress test. The results therefore do not yet establish performance
across many tasks, models, or prompt distributions. Third, the interpretation
stage depends on existing feature descriptions, so summary labels inherit errors
or missing information from those descriptions.

\section{Conclusion}
This thesis formalized the problem of summarizing attribution graphs and
proposed an automated pipeline for producing compact, human-readable summary
graphs. The method replaces manual pruning and manual grouping with explicit
objectives: retaining important nodes and edges, grouping features with similar
causal roles, preserving visible directed structure, and assigning labels to the
resulting supernodes. The output is a directed acyclic summary graph that keeps
the prompt, the predicted output, and the main signed pathways visible for
inspection.

The experiments show that this approach can produce summaries that remain
faithful at the graph level, improve structural coherence over several
baselines, and preserve much of the signed intervention behavior of the original
attribution graph. These results support the central claim of the thesis:
attribution graphs can be compressed into smaller mechanistic summaries without
discarding the causal information needed for inspection.

\section{Future Work}

Future work should strengthen both the method and its evaluation. A first
direction is to replace the current SHAP-based relevance proxy with a relevance
measure defined directly on the attribution graph, so that upstream and
downstream importance are expressed in the same linear framework. A second
direction is to evaluate the pipeline on more models, tasks, and prompt types,
especially cases where circuits involve longer reasoning chains or multiple
competing answers. A third direction is to improve label validation, either by
measuring whether supernode labels predict member-feature behavior or by using
intervention results to test whether a labeled supernode behaves as a single
mechanism. Finally, the visualization interface can be extended so that
researchers can move between the raw attribution graph, the pruned graph, and
the summary graph during analysis.

\section{Chapter Summary}

This chapter interpreted the empirical findings and stated the remaining
limitations of the proposed summarization method. The results indicate that
influence--relevance pruning, objective-driven clustering, and supernode
labeling can turn a large attribution graph into a smaller summary graph while
preserving important graph-level and intervention-level behavior. The main
limitations are the use of a proxy relevance score, the narrow quantitative
evaluation setting, and the dependence on existing feature labels. The thesis
therefore concludes that automated attribution-graph summarization is a viable
step toward scalable circuit inspection, while leaving broader validation and
tighter attribution objectives as future work.
