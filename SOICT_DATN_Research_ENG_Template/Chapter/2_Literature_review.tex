The previous chapter established that an attribution graph gives a faithful, mechanistic account of how a language model produces a single prediction, but that inspecting such graphs by hand does not scale, which motivates an automated method for summarizing them. Designing that method requires a precise grasp of the objects it manipulates and the techniques it builds upon, and this chapter supplies that background. It first bounds the scope of the work (Section~\ref{sec:scope}), then reviews the transformer architecture (Section~\ref{sec:transformer}) and the concepts of mechanistic interpretability (Section~\ref{sec:mech-interp}), the sparse autoencoders and transcoders that recover interpretable features (Section~\ref{sec:sae-transcoder}), and the circuit-tracing backbone of Ameisen et al.~\cite{ameisen2025circuit} that produces the attribution graphs this thesis summarizes (Section~\ref{sec:circuit-tracing}). It closes with the automated interpretation methods that supply feature labels (Section~\ref{sec:autointerp}) and a review of related work on describing and summarizing circuits (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

\section{Scope of Research}
\label{sec:scope}
This thesis studies the post-hoc mechanistic interpretation of a single decoder-only language model through the attribution graph of one prompt at a time. It takes the replacement model and the raw attribution graph of Ameisen et al.~\cite{ameisen2025circuit} as given, and concentrates on summarizing that graph into a compact, faithful, and human-readable form. Training the underlying transcoders, recovering circuits that run through attention patterns, and reasoning jointly across many prompts at once all lie outside its scope. Feature meanings are taken from existing Neuronpedia descriptions rather than generated anew.

\section{Transformer}
\label{sec:transformer}
% Tiêu đề và nội dung của chương này sẽ thay đổi tuỳ thuộc vào từng đồ án. Chú ý trình bày những kiến thức có liên quan mật thiết nhất đối với đồ án của mình, Tránh trình bày lan man những kiến thức phổ thông không cần thiết.
% Reference paper: https://transformer-circuits.pub/2021/framework/index.html
The transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention} maps a sequence of token embeddings to next-token predictions through a stack of attention and MLP sublayers. The decoder-only models studied here share one structural property that makes them amenable to mechanistic analysis: every sublayer reads from a shared \emph{residual stream} and adds its output back into it instead of overwriting it (Figure~\ref{fig:transformer}). The representation at any depth is therefore the sum of the original embedding and the contributions of all preceding sublayers, and the output logits are a linear read-out of this sum. This additive structure is what allows a later computation to be attributed back to the specific earlier contributions that produced it, and it is the structure the replacement model of Section~\ref{sec:circuit-tracing} exploits. The internal mechanics of attention and the MLP are not needed in what follows; we refer the reader to the original work for them.

\section{Mechanistic Interpretability and Circuits}
\label{sec:mech-interp}
Mechanistic interpretability is a subfield of explainable AI that seeks to reverse-engineer a model's internal computation into human-interpretable mechanisms, recovering the algorithm the model implements rather than only relating its inputs to its outputs. Its basic unit is the \emph{feature}: a property of the input that the model computes and reuses across contexts, such as a concept or a syntactic role. The \emph{linear representation hypothesis} holds that a feature is a direction in activation space whose strength is the magnitude of the activation along it~\cite{elhage2021framework}; this is natural for transformers, whose computation is dominated by linear maps on the residual stream, so meaningful properties can be encoded as directions and recovered by projection.

A \emph{circuit} is a subgraph of the model's computation that maps earlier features to later ones through the specific weights and non-linearities used in that mapping~\cite{olah2020zoom}. Features compose hierarchically, with early layers computing simple features that later layers combine into more abstract ones, and the residual stream lets a circuit span non-adjacent layers by carrying features forward unchanged. Recovering circuits expressed over interpretable features is the central goal of this thesis, because such a circuit explains not merely \emph{what} a model predicts but \emph{how} the prediction is computed.

The obstacle to reading circuits directly off neurons is \emph{superposition}: a model represents more features than it has dimensions, packing them as an overcomplete set of nearly orthogonal directions in activation space~\cite{elhage2022superposition}. A direct consequence is \emph{polysemanticity}, where a single neuron activates for several unrelated features and so admits no single interpretation. Superposition is an efficient compression strategy for the model but a barrier for interpretation, because the computational basis of neurons no longer aligns with the representational basis of features. Overcoming this misalignment is what the dictionary-learning methods of the next section are designed to do.
\section{Sparse Autoencoders and Transcoders}
\label{sec:sae-transcoder}
% The purpose of this section is to introduce the concepts of sparse autoencoders and transcoders, and to explain how they can be used to recover interpretable features and circuits from a model. The section is organised into two parts: the first part defines sparse autoencoders and their training objective; the second part introduces transcoders as a variant of sparse autoencoders that can be used to recover feature-to-feature interactions, and explains how they enable the circuit-tracing pipeline of the next section.
Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) address the polysemanticity barrier by learning a representational basis that is aligned with features rather than neurons~\cite{cunningham2023sparse}. Framed as dictionary learning, an SAE encodes a model activation $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ into a sparse, overcomplete code $c = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\mathrm{enc}} x + b)$ with far more entries than $d$, and reconstructs the activation from that code as $\hat{x} = W_{\mathrm{dec}} c$. Training minimises the reconstruction error $\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert_2^2$ together with an $\ell_1$ sparsity penalty $\alpha \lVert c \rVert_1$, in which $\alpha$ trades reconstruction fidelity against sparsity. Because only a few code units are active at a time, each unit can specialise to a single feature, and the resulting dictionary directions are substantially more monosemantic and interpretable than individual neurons. SAEs thus recover, in an unsupervised way, the feature directions that superposition had hidden.

SAEs nevertheless remain awkward for circuit analysis, because they are trained to reconstruct one activation in place and say nothing directly about how features at one site cause features at another. A \emph{transcoder} is a variant designed to close this gap~\cite{dunefsky2024transcoders}: instead of reconstructing its input, it predicts the \emph{output} of an MLP sublayer from that sublayer's \emph{input}. With the same sparse encoder $z(x) = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\mathrm{enc}} x + b_{\mathrm{enc}})$ and a decoder $\mathrm{TC}(x) = W_{\mathrm{dec}} z(x) + b_{\mathrm{dec}}$, a transcoder is trained to approximate the MLP map,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TC}}(x) = \lVert \mathrm{MLP}(x) - \mathrm{TC}(x) \rVert_2^2 + \lambda \lVert z(x) \rVert_1 .
\end{equation}
By replacing the MLP with a sparse linear map over interpretable features, the transcoder \emph{skips over} the MLP non-linearity that otherwise blocks attribution. The effect of one feature on another then factorises into an input-dependent term, the source activation, and an input-invariant term given by the dot product between the source decoder direction and the target encoder direction, which depends only on the learned weights. This factorisation is what makes feature-to-feature edges cheap and faithful to compute, and it is the property the circuit-tracing pipeline of the next section is built upon.
\section{Circuit Tracing Backbone}
\label{sec:circuit-tracing}
This thesis takes as its input the attribution graphs produced by the circuit-tracing methodology of Ameisen et al.~\cite{ameisen2025circuit}, so this section describes that methodology at the level needed to understand what the summarization pipeline consumes. It proceeds in three parts: the replacement model that re-expresses the transformer as a linear network over interpretable features; the attribution graph built from that model for a single prompt; and the interventions that test whether the graph is faithful. The pruning and supernode grouping steps that this thesis automates appear here only as the manual baseline they replace, their formal treatment being deferred to the methodology chapter.

\subsection{Replacement Model}
The replacement model re-expresses the underlying transformer as a network whose computation runs over interpretable features rather than neurons, so that the effect of one feature on another can be read off directly. It is built from cross-layer transcoders and is then specialized to a single prompt.

\subsubsection{Cross-Layer Transcoders}
A cross-layer transcoder (CLT) extends the single-layer transcoder of Section~\ref{sec:sae-transcoder} so that a feature can act on several layers at once~\cite{ameisen2025circuit}. As before, features read from the residual stream through a sparse encoder, but a feature that activates at layer $\ell'$ now writes to the output of its own MLP \emph{and} of every later MLP. Writing $x^\ell$ for the residual stream entering the MLP of layer $\ell$, the feature activations and the reconstructed MLP output are
\begin{align}
a^\ell &= \mathrm{JumpReLU}\!\left(W_{\mathrm{enc}}^\ell\, x^\ell\right), \label{eq:clt-enc}\\
\hat{y}^\ell &= \sum_{\ell' \le \ell} W_{\mathrm{dec}}^{\ell' \to \ell}\, a^{\ell'}. \label{eq:clt-dec}
\end{align}
A single CLT is trained to reconstruct every MLP output across the model under a sparsity penalty, leaving on the order of a hundred active features per token. Because a feature can deposit its effect many layers downstream without an intervening reconstruction step, the CLT recovers genuinely cross-layer features and avoids the near-duplicate features that a stack of independent single-layer transcoders would produce at each layer.

\subsubsection{From Global to Local Replacement Model}
The global replacement model substitutes the CLT for every MLP in the network while leaving the attention layers untouched, giving a single model that computes over features at every layer. This substitution is imperfect: the CLT does not reconstruct the MLP outputs exactly, and the resulting model reproduces the original next-token prediction on only about half of pretraining-style prompts. The global model is therefore not analyzed directly; it serves as the starting point for a prompt-specific construction in which the two remaining sources of nonlinearity are removed.

For a fixed prompt, the local replacement model freezes the two input-dependent nonlinear components --- the attention patterns and the LayerNorm normalization denominators --- to the values they took on the original forward pass, and adds an \emph{error node} at each (layer, token) position equal to the difference between the true MLP output and the CLT reconstruction there. The error terms make the construction exact: with them, the local replacement model reproduces the underlying model's activations and logits on that prompt. The freezing makes it linear: once attention and normalization are constants, each feature's preactivation is an affine function of the upstream feature activations, so a feature's input is simply the sum of its incoming edges. This linearity is the property the rest of the methodology rests on, because it lets the effect of one feature on another be attributed exactly rather than estimated.

\subsection{Attribution Graphs}
An attribution graph is the directed graph of feature-to-feature effects that the local replacement model induces for one prompt. Its nodes are the active features and the boundary terms of that model, and its edges are the direct linear effects between them.

\subsubsection{Nodes}
The graph has four kinds of nodes. \emph{Feature nodes} are the active CLT features, each tied to a (layer, token) position and carrying an interpretable meaning; they are the nodes that the summarization pipeline groups. \emph{Embedding nodes} hold the prompt's token embeddings and act as primary inputs with no incoming edges. \emph{Error nodes} hold the per-position reconstruction residuals; like embedding nodes they are sources with no incoming edges, but they carry no interpretation and stand for the part of the computation the CLT failed to capture. \emph{Logit nodes} represent the candidate output tokens and act as sinks with no outgoing edges. The methodology chapter formalizes these four node types as the vertex set on which the summary-graph problem is defined.

\subsubsection{Edges and Edge Weights}
An edge measures the direct linear effect of a source node on a target feature's preactivation through the frozen local replacement model, where ``direct'' means the immediate interaction, with any effect mediated by other features counted as separate edges. Because the model is linear on a fixed prompt, this effect factorizes into the prompt-dependent source activation and an input-invariant virtual weight,
\begin{equation}
A_{s \to t} = a_s\, w_{s \to t}. \label{eq:edge-weight}
\end{equation}
The virtual weight $w_{s \to t}$ is the dot product of the source feature's decoder vector with the target feature's encoder vector, summed over the layers at which the source writes ahead of the target; for an embedding source the embedding vector replaces the decoder, and for a logit target the unembedding replaces the encoder. Since $a_s$ depends on the prompt while $w_{s \to t}$ depends only on the trained weights, each edge is cheap to evaluate and faithful to the model's computation. Collecting these signed weights over all node pairs gives the weighted adjacency matrix that the next chapter takes as its input.

\subsubsection{Pruning and Supernode Grouping in the Original Method}
The raw attribution graph is too large to read: it holds thousands of feature nodes joined by a dense web of edges, most of which carry negligible weight. Ameisen et al. reduce it in two manual steps. They first prune the graph, keeping only the nodes and edges that account for most of the indirect influence on the logits and discarding the rest. They then group the surviving features into supernodes by inspection, collecting features that play the same role into a single labelled unit so the circuit can be drawn as a small diagram. Both steps rely on human judgment and do not scale: pruning thresholds are tuned by hand, and grouping a single circuit can take hours of expert effort. Automating exactly these two steps is the contribution of this thesis; the methodology chapter formalizes pruning as the pruning stage (Section~\ref{sec:stage1}) and supernode grouping as the clustering and interpreting stages (Sections~\ref{sec:stage2}--\ref{sec:stage3}).

\subsection{Interventions}
An attribution graph is a hypothesis about how the model computes its output, and the same local replacement model supplies the means to test it. The central tool is \emph{constrained activation patching}: a feature's activation is set to a chosen value over the narrow layer window in which it acts, the forward pass is resumed from that point, and the resulting changes in other features and in the output logits are measured. Steering a feature --- for example multiplying its activation by a negative factor to suppress it --- and checking that the downstream effects match those the graph predicts confirms that the recovered edges describe genuine causal pathways rather than coincidental correlations. These interventions are how the faithfulness of a circuit is established, and this thesis reuses them in its evaluation to verify that the supernodes of a summary graph behave as the single mechanisms their labels claim.
\section{Automated Interpretation Methods}
\label{sec:autointerp}
Recovering a feature direction leaves open what it means, and labelling features by hand does not scale to the millions a dictionary can contain. Automated interpretation (auto-interp) assigns each unit a natural-language description with a large language model: an \emph{explainer} model writes a description from the unit's top-activating examples, and a \emph{simulator} model is then scored on how well that description predicts the unit's activations on held-out text~\cite{bills2023language}. Because the score rewards descriptions that capture the true activation pattern, auto-interp succeeds far better on the monosemantic features of sparse autoencoders and transcoders than on polysemantic neurons, whose many unrelated activations no single sentence can describe. This thesis does not run auto-interp itself; it consumes the resulting public feature labels through Neuronpedia to name the supernodes of a summary graph.

\section{Related Works}
\label{sec:related}
The work closest to this thesis automates the interpretation of attribution graphs directly. ADAG~\cite{arora2026adag} uses a language model to generate natural-language descriptions of an attribution graph, replacing the manual labelling that the original methodology relies on. Its strength is that it removes the human bottleneck in naming what a graph contains. It is, however, complementary to the problem addressed here: ADAG describes a graph whose structure is taken as given, whereas this thesis reduces that structure, pruning and grouping the graph into a small summary before any labelling. Description and structural summarization solve different halves of the readability problem.

A parallel line of work questions the substrate on which circuits are built. Arora et al.~\cite{arora2026sparse} show that circuits traced directly in the neuron basis can be as sparse and as faithful as those built from transcoder features, avoiding transcoder training, reconstruction error, and the associated error nodes. The approach is attractive for those reasons, but the authors observe that the resulting circuits remain too large for a human to read and call for principled grouping methods to compress them. That is precisely the bottleneck this thesis targets, and the summarization method developed here is agnostic to the substrate: it operates on any signed attribution graph, whether its nodes are transcoder features or neurons.

Summarizing large directed graphs is also studied outside interpretability. Causal DAG summarization~\cite{youngmann2025causal} compresses the causal graphs used in causal inference while preserving their salient relationships, offering principled objectives for graph summarization. Those objectives do not transfer directly, because a causal-inference DAG encodes relationships among external variables rather than signed, feature-to-feature effects within a model on a specific prompt, and it carries no notion of faithfulness to an underlying network. Across these lines of work, no existing method produces a compact and faithful structural summary of an attribution graph by jointly pruning and clustering its feature nodes under an explicit faithfulness objective; supplying one is the aim of this thesis.

This chapter assembled the background the summarization pipeline depends on, following a single line of reasoning. Superposition hides a model's features inside its neurons; sparse autoencoders and transcoders recover those features as interpretable directions; and the cross-layer transcoders of the circuit-tracing backbone turn them into a per-prompt attribution graph whose edges are exact linear effects between features. That attribution graph is the object this thesis summarizes. The review also located the gap the thesis fills: the original methodology prunes and groups the graph by hand, while related work either describes graphs without compressing them or produces circuits that are still too large to read. Automated structural summarization of an attribution graph therefore remains open, and the next chapter formalizes that problem and presents the three-stage method --- pruning, clustering, and interpreting --- that solves it.
